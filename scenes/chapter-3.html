Chapter 3

To This Point

Back home, Judah is pulled into both the investigation and the family wreckage he left behind. Crime scene photos suggest the attack was not a simple animal mauling, and Dr. Margaret Jones managed to leave a cryptic dying message. Temperance confronts Judah over fifteen years of absence, but they reach a fragile truce. In Isaac’s study, Judah finds a threatening fax about a “Watcher,” and strange details begin piling up faster than reason can explain them.

Temperance

I stomped up the porch steps to the side door, making my presence known and apparent. At this point on a Thursday, my nephew, Tyler would be at school, so I just needed to make sure Temper didn’t actually shoot me not knowing who was coming through the door. I knocked as I twisted the handle, it was unlocked in either anticipation of my arrival, or the novel rural nativity that allowed for an unlocked door to not even be a second thought.

It was a lot… smaller than I had remembered, a living room opening up into a small kitchen, with a hallway going to the left in front of a pair of bedrooms leading to a bathroom at the end of the hallway. The house was only dimly lit by the sunlight outside, filtered through lace priscilla curtains that my great grandmother had picked out and bought for my grandfather when they had built this house. Though, now instead of the pristine white they’d started to show their age with sunbaked yellows staining the light as it diffused into the room. I counted a third and fourth door that I hadn’t recognized but figured that Doc Isaac must have had it added to the house when Temperance and Tyler began living with him full-time after the divorce. I didn’t see anyone home though, which was strange for having given Ryan such a hard time about taking me anywhere else.

I sighed and wandered back to the kitchen area of the home dimly lit by the indirect pressing sunlight from outside. I posted up, resting the back of my hips against the countertops and resting my palms on the cool Formica surface, worn smooth from decades of hands, elbows, and other casual contact with the occupants of the home through the years. The counter squeaked under my weight, the different materials protesting and welcoming a new occupant to its decrepit hallowed perch. I looked up, seeing my reflection in a mirror hung in the living room, and saw my father. Not actually, but seeing myself in this pose, I hadn’t realized how much I carried of him until now, standing here in the same pose, hands braced against the same counters. He’d spent hours like this, his thoughts locked away behind a furrowed brow. How many times had I watched him and wondered what he was chewing over, only to become the man doing the same without even knowing it?

After several minutes, Temperance Bennett-Sands finally came out to the living area of the house. She had on soft sweatpants and a baggy tee shirt, her coppery hair bundled in a wet mass of bun like she’d just from taking a shower.

“Judah!-“ she started, her eyes flashing in surprise and then anger.

I threw my hands up in apologetic surrender. “That’s my bad sorry, I should have called out as opposed to getting… sucked into my own thoughts.”

Her eyes narrowed, deeper into anger and resentment. “Nice to see you home-” her tone dripped of buttered cyanide, “-you are a sight for sore eyes, even though it only took potentially your father being killed to get you to come by and at least pretend to give a damn.”

“Excuse-“ I said, rising out of my chair before the little demon cut me off.

“Excuse what? Fifteen years Judah. You book out of here the first chance that came up, not that anyone could blame you there, but then you didn’t look back for FIFTEEN years. That’s older than my son Judah. That’s longer than my marriage to your brother, Judah. That’s a whole-ass high schooler of abandonment that you put me and the rest of this family through, and I’m this mad about it and I’m not even the one related to you.” Her eyes were flashing daggers now, and each sentence brought her closer to me until her tiny frame was so close that I felt the need to take a step back from the radiating heat of her indignation. “Where were you when Levi decided that me wanting to go into a master’s program to help your dad was a good enough reason to jump into the drug scene, Judah? Where were you the first, I don’t know, five or ten times that he’d pulled himself out that scene Judah, or when he fell back in, or when I was here, caring for a baby while having no clue what ditch my husband had passed out in? Or when he couldn’t get or keep a job because of his habit?” Well, if the universe wasn’t enough for twisting screws in my guilt, Temper certainly didn’t have a problem helping it out some.

Now I could see the tears forming as she raised her voice and pitched even higher. “NOW YOU COME BACK? NOW YOU WANT TO HELP? NOW YOU GIVE A DAMN?” Her voice cracked. “What gives you any right, Judah Sands? What gives you the nerve?”

I am a big strong man. I’ve stood and stared down drill sergeants screaming obscenities, without so much as a blink to let them know they’d hit anywhere close to home. However, with this 100 lb titan of terror reading me a list of my sins, I crumbled like the proverbial house upon the sand. Leaning back against the wall of the kitchen I sank to a sitting position on the floor. I sat there for the moment, the stillness of the house only broken by the sound of Temperance's tear streaked heaving breaths, her energy spent in her emotional explosion. The silence lingered between us, growing like so much mildew on the exterior of the house, like it's bones knew the rot of this family before even we did.

“I don’t have the right. I know that. I don’t have the right to walk into your home expecting any sort of welcome outside of a thrown cast iron. I fucked up Temp." She glared, the familiarity of her nickname not going unnoticed. "--I know that you sure as hell know it, I’m sure my dad and Levi also know it.” I sighed, looking around the room, and feeling the near musty environment of decay and academia coming through even Temperance's fastidious protectiveness of her son's environment. Around the smell of cleaners you could smell the hallmarks of age, dusty, maybe the acrid tang of mold from broken pipes years ago finally taking root in the small corners of the unreplaced cabinetry. The wood swollen and warped like every promise I’d made and broken. My father had asked me to fix them years ago, and I’d walked away. Just like I’d walked away from all of this. “I can’t make it right, and I can’t come back and be here to be a source of comfort and support over the last decade and a half. But maybe, I can help Dad.”

That prompted Temper to sit, hugging her knees to her chest as we both sat on the cool black and white checkered linoleum of the kitchen floor. She inhaled deeply and locked eyes on me. "That's... not how I wanted that to play out." She sighed, the bomb apparently defusing a bit. "It's been, a rough day. Luckily Tyler was feeling better enough to go to school so I could sit with it a while, and not have to keep up the positive mom face." She sat her chin on her knees.

"I can imagine that would have been rough."

"You'd have to."

Cruel. But an accurate statement that just twisted the knife in the guilt more. "I don't know how much Ryan has filled you in, but from what they could gather, Dad wasn't one of the ones they found up there."

"I mean, if it was an animal, couldn't it have dragged him off to eat him elsewhere?" She asked, a gloom creeping into her voice.

"You aren't wrong, but looking at the crime scene photos--" this isn't like any animal attack that I've ever seen. And I've lived somewhere that you could actually get Animal Planet." My attempted joke landed flat and she glared daggers at me as I held up my hands in surrender.

"I had to try, it’s a reflex in stressful situations. Ryan is waiting to hear back from the coroner in Jewel Hill, which might give us some more clues about who these folks were and what exactly happened to them. Hopefully, with that information, Ryan'll be more willing to let me go up to the scene with him to try to get a timeline on the attack."

"I'm coming with you guys."

"What about-"

"I'll call my sister and have her come watch him, but I didn't go with Isaac and this happened, so you best believe I'm not going to be responsible for leaving two Sands on that mountain."

The Investigation Gains Steam

It took more than a few hours of going through my father's effects before I started developing the rhythm. The first and hardest part, as tended to be the case with my father, was to organize the information at hand so I knew what I was looking at, for, and with. To amuse myself, I came across a trio of statues tucked among the dozens of little baubles tucked among the books on the dusty and rough shelves and on the flat surfaces of low tables. A sextant he'd purchased on a trip in Portugal, an idol from a marketplace in the Yucatan, a replica organ container from Cairo, he was never satisfied with a trip unless he had something tangible to remember it by. That thought led to a mild burn starting behind my eyes, I wasn't one to be that in touch with my feelings, but at a guess this was the overwhelming sadness that would cause people in a similar situation to have the moments of despondence that I couldn't afford. I dragged in a deep and suspiciously ragged breath, forcing the feeling into the box so I could work. I guess in the present circumstance he'd brought something tangible for us to experience from him. Since for the moment this was my office until we found him alive and well. I picked one, the looked to be a period farmer with a rifle slung high on his shoulder at the ready, his eyes shielded beneath a broad brimmed hat, and decided he looked like what I would associate as someone who was "Looking For". A lost calf, a son, prey on a hunt. I smiled to myself at the comparison. Not a farmer. A hunter. I placed him on the far left of the hutch of the desk, right above a paper sized cubby, shuffling a stack of papers into the void under the figurine. These were documents that referenced things like "Gwynporth" which seemed to be some kind of location that Corven and my Father thought was near to the town, and "Drywds" which just operating by cognate logic suggested that my father and the Cryptozoological mogul were looking for Druids in these mountains which struck me as more preposterous than even dad would take out for a lark. Another, an older looking figurine of a slighter man, knelt with a spyglass seemed very appropriate for a "Looking For" and I set him to the center of the desk. Something about the figurine peering through the spyglass seemed appropriate for what I was going for. Under "For" I had my legal pad with a series of questions written down on it "Where was dad going?", "Did they find the site they were looking for?" etc. The final one I set on the hutch above the laptop, A matronly figure, dressed in a long dress and apron, much akin to what the old folks would call a Granny, with a basket of herbs crooked in her arm. I deemed her as "Looking With" for the trio, and anything I brought to the desk to try to solve this mystery was under her domain. Pleased with my organizational in-joke, I pulled papers from the “For” cubby. They were a shabby mix—folded corners, bent edges, and brittle pages bearing the marks of long years spent scattered on my father’s desk. Some were older reference materials, photocopied from books stored in climate-controlled archives my father had once frequented. Others were newer, cross-referenced against the overstuffed shelves that groaned under the weight of his personal library. I sat back in my father’s—no, my—creaking bank chair, its metallic springs straining with each shift. The seat groaned in harmony with the faint squeal of the ceiling fan overhead, stirring the dead, stale air. The study felt like something between a library and a warehouse—part intellectual haven, part storeroom for treasures that might have been plucked from Raiders of the Lost Ark. The expansive executive desk, worn and imposing, anchored the room. My father had bought it when he retired from academia, a relic of his transition from professor to full-time adventurer. I opened its drawers, the wood sticking slightly as though reluctant to give up its secrets. The muscles in my face twitched a little as I saw the paper on the top of the stack in one of the drawers. It was clean and white, like it was a fresh copy, but I could see the header and footer lines that told me it was a faxed document to my father. I glanced at the printer that my nephew had told me they had recently set up in the office, and sure enough from the angle to it I was, I could see the phone jack plugged into the side, allowing for faxes to come through. The image on the document was, alarming. A scrawled hand written note that said "THE SEEKER IS WATCHED. THE WATCHER WAITS." I read the page twice, and a third time, the hair on my neck raising with each successive read. Was dad being threatened? Who had sent this? The letters themselves were scrawled and jagged, like each stroke was a threat made into graphic. I looked at the backside of the paper. Blank. Except for a small header note on that side that had the number that the image had been faxed from, and a date, 10/16/25. Just the day before my father left on his expedition. That couldn't be a coincidence. The hum of the ceiling fan droned on over my thoughts, along with it's periodic squeak of protest from moving the still all too still air in the room. The clatter from the window was an explosion of sound. Heavy, Sharp, Metallic. As if someone had thrown something through the glass of the window. I jerked upright out of the bankers chair, sending it rolling away from the back of my legs as I drew a bead on the source of the noise, my service weapon in my hand without the line of thoughts to instruct my hands and arms to do so. My ears thumping with my own heart beat and adrenaline laced blood. Heart beating against my ribs, I moved closer to the window to find the source of the noise. As I got close enough to the glass, I saw my own reflection, eyes flared open, the whites standing out starkly as I stared. Then another flash of movement drew my attention. A blur of blue, grey, black and white plumage came to a stop on the window sill and stared back at me through the glass. A blue jay. Breathing heavily, I holstered the gun. With the amount of weirdness going on, and a targeted threat to my father, it seemed like I couldn't be too safe I guess and my defensive reflexes went off. I stared at the Jay and it stared back at me, seemingly unphased by me being just on the other side of the glass from it. It cocked it's head, almost inviting me to speak, and before I could think about anything "...Velandor?" escaped from my lips. I heard the 'keue' of the Blue Jay respond approvingly through the window, as it flitted away, back into the darkness. I shook my head. "What is a Velandor?" I pondered to the empty room, my hands moving to the bracelet on my wrist, rubbing the Amethyst embedded in the leather with a practiced preoccupation. The stone had come from my mother's necklace after she'd died, not that I was particularly superstitious, but it felt nice to have a physical connection to her still. I found it soothing. I glanced back at the fax, now sitting on the floor from the interruption. Why did the Watcher and this mysterious unbidden word seem connected. I couldn't wrap words around it as to why, but something instinctual screamed at me from deep inside that these two things were connected. I rubbed at the stone some more in thought, feeling the cool surface under my fingertips. I shook my head and cleared my mind from the distraction. I couldn't afford it. I crouched and picked up the fax, careful to keep it in the same condition that I'd found it. I stood at the desk and jotted down the fax number from the header of the sheet onto my legal pad, and then located the chair I'd been using and drew it back under me as i booted on the laptop. Let's see if I could figure out who had sent this fax.

It took some doing, but I eventually found a listing on a message board that directed me to a source for the number on the fax header. It had been for a "S. Swan Thirdhand Thrift and Goode Co." that was listed in Walnut. I'd just jotted down the number when I heard the soft, deliberate padding footsteps of Temperance approaching the door to the study. The door creaked open, the hinges sounding like they had been original to a much older house than this, the metal squealing as it grinded against itself to allow entry. Temperance for her part was put together for the day, her curly copper hair tied up in a long ponytail behind her over her hoodie, her left hand holding a plate with a sandwich aloft. "I might be mad, but I'm not going to let someone starve under my roof." She said curtly as she crossed the room to the edge of the desk to set down the plate. "What are you working-" She stopped cold as she saw the fax, and the scrawled words upon it. "What the hell? What is this supposed to be?" I sighed, drumming my fingers on the legal pad next to the laptop. Damn it all I was tired. "Either a warning, or a threat, sent two days before Dad headed out." I looked up and locked eyes with Temperance. "Best I can figure out the seeker is meant to be Dad, and I'm not sure who the Watcher is referring to. I hate to be like this, but has anyone been, I don't know, threatening dad more broadly than an anonymous fax?" Temperance pursed her lips, and knitted her brows in annoyance. "Two Days? Was it still on the printer or-?" "No, it was tucked in one of the drawers. So I assume Dad did what Dad does, and saw it and ignored it." I raised my hands in exasperation and leaned back in the chair. "Probably assumed it was a crank and nothing would come of it." "Of course he did. I mean, if you wanted a narrow list of people that would threaten him, you are out of luck." Her annoyance made the jab clear in her voice, even if the appended "Because you weren't here to know about it" wasn't stated. "Probably not the Puritans over in Town of Joe, they'd not have bothered with the fax and just done something like try to ruin Isaac's reputation, then it's maybe another online Cryptid Hunter that he debunked a video of them saying something about their favorite cryptid. If not them, it's someone around here who he's pissed off by being either too involved in local goings on or not involved enough depending on the person. He wasn't exactly someone that minded the Ps and Qs of the status quo around here Judah." I nodded. That had always been true of dad, both when I was a kid and after I got out into the world. He'd never been one to let anyone else dictate how he felt, or talked about a subject. "Well, one, Puritans? I thought they were in Massachusetts, and two, So not narrowing it down to less than half the county. Lets add a variable. What about the--" I stole a glance to the legal pad to make sure I got the name right, "--Swan Thirdhand Thrift and Goode Co. With an E?" Temperance blinked. "Swan? The old guy in Walnut? I don't think he ever seemed to have a beef with him. Isaac's been going over there a lot lately though. Right after this expedition contract landed. Maybe he though Mister Stefan knew something?" "I mean maybe. I don't actually know a lot about him, Grandpa visited him with me a couple of times, but I usually was distracted by all the stuff in there and didn't really pay attention to anything they were saying to each other." I frowned. I shouldn't blame child me for not paying attention, what child would pay attention to what two old men were gossiping about. Especially in the presence of nigh ancient bric-a-brac lining every wall and shelf, begging to be taken down and played with. "Well, I ask because that's what I found this number tied to. According to the 'Dusty Vintage Relics' message board, someone has said it's the fax number to the place." Temperance smiled a bemused smile. "I mean, Mister Stefan is pretty old, I can't think of anyone that would still have a working fax number." She shot a look to the pristine printer, standing out as the new marvel of technology among my father's effects in the library. "Outside of Isaac apparently." I smirked at the jab towards my father. "So, operating on them being friends, or at least business friendly, I'd think this would be more a warning than a threat. I think I need to go up there and see Swan for myself." Temperance quirked an ill-tempered eyebrow. "Judah, are you sure that's a good idea? Not like you've got a healthy reputation around here at all, and there's only so far that Isaac's coattails can take you." She shifted her weight and cocked her head her expression softening from a steely sarcasm to something more akin to comradery and concern, while the shifting of her head sent the waves of copper from her ponytail shimmering over her shoulder. "Not that it's an unhealthy one, just it doesn't really exist at all around here." She looked down at the sandwich that remained untouched on the plate. "Also eat. You aren't going anywhere until you take care of yourself some and stop looking like a half starved possum that wandered in from the woods." I begrudgingly lifted the sandwich and took a bite. The taste of baloney and mustard filled my mouth, which caused a deep gurgling reaction in my stomach. How long had it been since I'd eaten? I chewed thoughtfully and swallowed. "Yeah, I guess I don't really have much of one, but maybe that will be more helpful than not. I also don't have the same rankles that Dad did with people. Or, more to the point, I'm not important enough to anyone to truly annoy them yet." I paused, taking another thoughtful bite of the sandwich. She wasn't wrong. I was basically a ghost in this town, more a name and memory rather than a person. Maybe I could work that to my advantage, or maybe it just meant no one would care what this sudden new Sands would uncover. I scarfed down more of the sandwich, and asked "So, this Stefan Swan, do you know him any?" Temperance slid off the desk and into one of the chairs. "Not very well. Enough to know that he's legit on his history. Old enough to have probably lived through most of it, at least according to him." she offered a rare wry smile to me. "He's not without his quirks, like Isaac. Swears by a lot of odd old stories. Knows more than he lets on. You might be surprised." I looked up as I finished my sandwich, against locking my eyes against Temperance's viridian ones. "Honestly, I'm not sure anyone up here surprises me at this point." I started to collect the papers I had questions on from around the desktop, neatly stacking them on the legal pad a couple of pages from the top. "But, if Stefan Swan has potential answers, I guess I need to go see him." Temperance stood up as I did and started heading for the door to the study. "Just don't try to scare him off. He might scare you back." she said. The lilt of a joke in the tone of her voice as she disappeared down the hallway. I picked up the keys to the Bronco and started striding towards the door myself. The watcher waits. Well if someone's watching. It's time they saw this seeker seek.

Judah Checks on Abraham

An hour or so later, I left the house again, heading up the hill to check in on my grandfather. Abraham Sands had been in an increasingly bad way for years, which was hardly surprising for a man in his early 90s. Dad’s messages had hinted at his growing disconnection from reality, his confusion. Now he was more or less bedridden, with a hospice nurse trying to keep him comfortable as he faded away—much like the town and most things in the high Appalachians.

Abraham’s house sat at the top of the same hill as my father’s, perched on the side of the mountain that shielded Deep Springs from the outside world. Modest, but enough for him, and before, for his two sons and my grandmother. For me and Levi, it had always been a second home, especially when Dad was off on expeditions. Back when my grandmother was alive, there were always beans, cornbread, and greens simmering on the stove, ready for anyone who might drop by—us, my parents, folks from town who’d grown up alongside Abraham, even the mayor or the chief of police. My grandfather was a hub of activity in town, just as his father had been before him.

As I turned into the loop road in front of his house, I thought of him with his pipe—a churchwarden he’d crafted himself as a young man. I could almost see the ghost of the grandfather of my youth, seated in one of the now-rusty metal chairs around the cliffside deck, gazing over the steep drop to the river and the patchwork of farms on the hills beyond. A regular Bilbo Baggins in the Shire, enjoying his golden years. The house itself was worn, as decrepit as the man who owned it was becoming, but still shadowed by the love and nostalgia I felt for it. The pang of twenty years lost beat at me as I stepped across the deck to the side door of the white building, under the faded red roof, that for all the world looked like a rusted ship hull sitting on bricks.

Taking a breath that held the faintest trace of pipe smoke from the annals of years before, I knocked on the door, and was met with a wait of a few minutes before the door was swung open and I was met by a surprise.

I knew my grandfather’s hospice nurse, I’d gone to school with her. Anna Caplan. “Oh my god, Judah?” She asked, looking up at me from her diminutive 5 foot height. The next thing I knew I was stumbling backwards as she hurried to give me a side hug. “Oh my god! Are you ok? I know things are crazy, I’ve been just trying to keep it from hitting Abe’s ear.” she glanced inside, and satisfied closed the door behind her. “I didn’t want to stress him too much until we knew more.” I gave a tired look. “I don’t know much I’m afraid, it doesn’t look great, but I’m hopeful. Dad’s a pretty resilient guy.”  Anna nodded approvingly of my charitable nature on the situation, and pushed open the door again “I guess you are here to see Abe. You picked an ok day for it, he’s not been that aware today, but he’s not screaming at the walls or anything, so that’s not for nothing.” I blinked at that, I hadn't realized he'd gotten so bad.

Abraham Sands, the patriarch of my family looked at me with glassy eyes, tracking the movement from the door in the odd juxtaposition of someone that wasn't entirely there, and a predator tracking prey. One foot in the door of death with his failing body, but the other in something, primal, maybe this is the sort of manifestation of the caged and trapped animal that some men become when death knocks. Maybe this is what dying of old age looks like.

"Judah? Judah my boy, you've come home" he rasped, his voice dry and strained like a man parched and half jerky after dragging themself through the desert. I crossed the room and nabbed up the water cup that was set next to his bed, quietly noting the Vanderbilt University logo printed on the side and holding it up to my grandfather's lips. I glanced back at Anna, who was checking linens in the closet. "I didn't realize Vanderbilt did at home care." She nodded at me. "They do, but that's not what this is. I'm my own practice I guess you could say." she smiled as she set fresh sheets at the foot of the medical bed. "The joys of being an entrepreneur I guess. The medical equipment-" she had caught my eyes flicking to the high dollar medical monitoring equipment that was attached to Abraham, "Is actually courtesy of your father. I don't know where he got them from, but they are newer than what I've had access to before, and he just gave them as "Payment in kind" to the practice. But yes, Vanderbilt is my alma mater, I just ran out of branded insulated cups, so that's one of mine." She laughed, a laugh that seemed as hollow as Abraham's physical form, one that hid self-consciousness and doubt. "All the high tech medical equipment, no money for the frivolities." She continued puttering around the living space, what i recalled to be Abraham's living room, his large cabinet TV, a hallmark of my memories of the home, covered in knick-knacks of my grandparents had been replaced with an electric hearth next to the old woodstove, with a flat screen mounted high to the wall above it for my grandfather's comfort of watching it in his bed (It was Wheel of Fortune, I didn't even know Wheel of Fortune ran at 1pm). The weathered old velveteen fabric couch, smelling of pipe smoke and years of tobacco and home cooking smells had been removed to make room for the medical apparatus around the room, converting the space into an ad hoc hospital accommodation. Anna noticed me again, looking around the room. "As different as it is from when we were kids, " I recalled periods where me and Ryan couldn't go anywhere without finding Anna nearby. She'd been the new kid in town, and as children are wont to be, several of our peers took every opportunity to try to improve their social station by reducing Anna, before me and Ryan took care of it in high school. After that, she'd take supper with my grandparents with me, Levi and Ryan, she'd come down to the river with us, or go trekking through the hills on grand adventures. Come to think of it, she was kind of nurse and caregiver to our little quartet as much as she seemed to be now. It's amazing sometimes how much nothing changes despite everything having changed. 'it's not much of a medical accommodation, but it works for him. It's best to keep his surroundings at least similar. Keeps him grounded during his... bad days ya know?" I didn't. Not really at least, but I nodded anyway. "He's been getting stuck on topics the last several days. Probably a week before--" She saw my confusion. "Word travels fastest in small towns remember? I know all about what happened on the trail, second, third, fourth hand at least." Her words hung in the air for a moment, heavy with unspoken implications. I opened my mouth to respond, but before I could form a thought, a low grumble came from the bed. “Dogs got ’em,” my grandfather rasped, his voice like dry leaves scraping together. Anna froze, her hands still clutching the edges of the sheet. “Oh boy, here we go,” she muttered, half to herself, her tone a mix of exasperation and concern. She turned toward Abraham, her movements careful, measured. “It’s been dogs and shadows when he’s having his moments,” she explained softly, glancing at me. “Sometimes he gets stuck on it for hours.” “Dogs and shadows?” I echoed, the chill in my voice betraying my attempt to sound unaffected. “Yeah. It’s always the same,” she said, stepping closer to the bed and lowering her voice, as though trying not to disturb him further. “They’re coming for him. Or for someone else. He doesn’t say who.” I looked down at my grandfather, whose eyes were still closed but whose lips moved faintly, murmuring words I couldn’t quite catch. A shiver crawled up my spine, and for a brief, irrational moment, I felt as though something unseen was watching from the corners of the room. “Dogs bite,” Abraham said suddenly, his voice sharper this time, startling both of us. His eyes opened, glassy and unfocused, but there was something in them—something feral and raw, as though he wasn’t really seeing us at all. “Scared dogs bite.”

Anna gave a small laugh, the sound more genuine this time, and returned to smoothing the sheets on Abraham’s bed. “Honestly, I’m not sure how much he notices anymore, but it makes me feel better. Like I’m doing something for him.”

Abraham shifted slightly in his bed, muttering something incoherent. His eyelids fluttered, but whatever lucidity he’d shown earlier had faded, leaving him slumped against the pillows, his breathing deep and rhythmic.

Anna leaned over to check the monitors, her movements quiet and careful. “Looks like he’s settled in for now,” she said softly. “This might be the best sleep he’s had all week.”

I watched him for a moment longer, my mind replaying his cryptic words. Dogs. Shadows. Scared dogs bite. A shiver ran through me, but I pushed it down. Just the ramblings of an old man slipping away, I told myself. It didn’t feel like a lie.

Anna straightened, catching my eye as she folded the last of the linens. “He’ll be alright for a while. You don’t need to stay if you’ve got other things to do.”

I hesitated, the weight of the day pressing against my chest. “Yeah. I need to head into town for a bit,” I said finally, forcing a small smile. “I’ll stop by again tomorrow.”

She smiled back, warm but faintly tired. “Good. He’ll like that. Even if he doesn’t show it.”

I stepped out onto the deck, pausing to glance back through the doorway. Abraham was already still, his breathing steady as the hum of the monitors filled the quiet. Anna busied herself tidying the room, her presence a calm anchor in the shifting tide of my thoughts. The faint scent of pipe smoke lingered in the air as I closed the door behind me. But even as the door closed, I could feel the pressure lingering on the back of my brain. Something old. Something I couldn’t quite place my finger on. For the first time, I noticed how the river below—cutting through the heart of the town—growled like a hunting animal, its roar of rushing water more alive than it should have been.

The center of town was bustling as I turned on my walk onto the main road. I was headed to the coffee shop I'd noticed with Ryan on the way into the town, both for the coffee and to see what this "Artisan Co-Op" was all about. The large metal sign stretched across the 50s-style brown brick building read "Moonbeam Mountain Coffeehouse"

Inside of the building could be called politely a cacophony of kitsch memorabilia. Posters, stickers, small trinkets like worry stones, gemstones, to larger table pieces like giant gemstone spheres, obelisks, and paintings. I grabbed a coffee from the counter and started to peruse. I owed my dad about 15 years worth of birthday presents anyway, so maybe something in here would be nice for his office. Something I didn't know what how well the scent of freshly brewed coffee would blend with cedarwood, patchouli and incense, creating an odd heady aroma that was as comforting as it was grounding. I'd been awake for about 10 hours, and felt foolish feeling run down, however in my defense, it'd been a hell of a 10 hours. A soft hum of folk music drifted from a corner where a local guitarist strummed and sang, her voice blending into the chatter of patrons scattered around mismatched tables and chairs. Each table seemed to have its own personality: some were bare wood, scarred from years of use, while others were painted with intricate designs, one featuring what looked like a hand-painted mural of the mountains at sunset.

I sipped the coffee—a rich, dark roast with a hint of something floral I couldn't quite place—and wandered past shelves crammed with treasures. Worry stones smoothed by countless hands caught the light, reflecting colors from a nearby stained-glass lamp. A sign read: "Locally Crafted by the Hands of the Appalachians" in wobbly, hand-painted script, and a piece of slate leaned against the shelf listing the artists’ names.

Further in, the shelves transitioned to larger items: gemstone obelisks that glittered like they held secrets, twisted driftwood sculptures, and paintings of ethereal landscapes. One caught my eye: a misty forest rendered in shades of green and gold, light pouring in like it was alive. The signature at the bottom was just two initials, the letters curling like vines.

My gaze snagged on a decorative skull perched on a tall wooden stand in the back corner. It was carved with strange, geometric designs and inlaid with a striking amethyst disc in the center of the forehead. My coffee halfway to my mouth, I paused, drawn toward it. It wasn’t just the artistry—though it was stunning—but something about it felt… familiar, though I’d never seen it before in my life.

Perfect, I thought. For Dad’s office. Definitely weird enough to get a comment, even from him.

I got the attention of the clerk in house, and had the case unlocked, the clerk gently passing me the skull to take to the counter at the front of the shop. I gasped as I took the skull in my arms, feeling a tingle of electric current almost coming from it. "Oh. That's wild." I said to no one in particular, moving to the desk and setting the skull down for purchase.

That accomplish, and the skull safely wrapped in newspaper and placed in a plastic bag I started back towards my fathers with my refilled coffee and new prize in tow.
